\section{Introduction}

Governance in multi-agent systems depends on the structure of interaction as well as the behaviour of individual agents. Recent work on agentic AI argues that capability may scale through interacting agents, services, and institutions instead of through one monolithic system \citep{evans2025agentic}. Under this view, alignment and oversight cannot be reduced to the behaviour of one system in isolation. They also depend on the rules and institutions that govern strategic interaction.

Sequential commons are useful for this purpose because they make a basic problem explicit. Individually beneficial behaviour can damage a shared system when the rules of interaction are weak or poorly designed \citep{gordon1954common, scott1955fishery, ostrom1990}. Work on sequential social dilemmas makes the same point in multi-agent settings: cooperation depends on temporally extended interaction, partner composition, and held-out social situations rather than only on one-shot payoffs \citep{leibo2017multiagent, meltingpot2021, socialjax2025}. More generally, individually aligned systems do not guarantee good collective outcomes once the structure of interaction is taken seriously \citep{conitzer2023foundations}.

Earlier Fishery Commons and Harvest Commons studies in the same project provide the immediate empirical background. Fishery Commons shows that central interventions can be ranked under repeated adversarial strategy injection in a minimal renewable commons. Harvest Commons shows that governance architecture matters more directly: local-only, central-only, and hybrid governance differ in how they trade ecological protection against local burden under stronger attacker pressure.

The present study re-evaluates Harvest with three literature-backed scenario archetypes and two oversight regimes. The scenario archetypes correspond to regulated fishery, community irrigation, and forest co-management. The oversight regimes introduce imperfect detection, delayed enforcement, limited targeting capacity, and governance budget cost. The aim is to examine how architecture rankings change once governance is placed in more realistic institutional settings.

The results vary across scenarios. Community-irrigation settings favour hybrid governance under both ideal and constrained oversight. Constrained forest co-management and the higher-pressure regulated-fishery setting favour top-down-only governance. The empirical contribution of the paper is therefore a scenario-dependent comparison of governance architecture under explicit oversight frictions.
